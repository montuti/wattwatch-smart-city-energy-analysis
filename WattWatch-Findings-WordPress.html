⚡ WattWatch: What Six Months of Smart-Meter Data Reveals About Urban Energy Use

An analysis of 72,400 daily smart-meter readings across five city zones (January–June 2025), built to find out where energy demand, cost, and grid reliability actually concentrate — and where the obvious answer turns out to be wrong.

1. North and Central Are the City's Demand Hotspots

Over six months, North zone consumed 260,776 kWh and Central consumed 245,377 kWh — both well ahead of South, the lowest-demand zone at just 184,304 kWh. East (222,803 kWh) and West (220,674 kWh) sit in between. The gap isn't just about meter count either: North has fewer meters than East but still uses more power per meter, pointing to a denser mix of commercial and industrial activity rather than simple scale.

2. Residential Uses the Most Energy Overall — Not Industrial

This is the finding that needed the most correction. An earlier pass at this data concluded that "Industrial consumers dominate total usage" — a reasonable-looking claim that turns out to be backwards.

So where did the original claim come from?

It's true that the five single highest-consuming meters in the whole dataset are all Industrial, each drawing 8,877–9,020 kWh over six months — 4 to 5 times a typical residential meter. But Industrial only accounts for about 10% of all meters (39 out of 400). A handful of very heavy meters doesn't add up to the largest category total when the category itself is small. Per-meter intensity and category-level demand are two different questions, and in this dataset they point in opposite directions.

💡 The practical takeaway:

If the goal is reducing total citywide energy use, Residential is the segment with the most room to move — not Industrial. Industrial is still the right target if the goal is capacity planning around a small number of very heavy sites.

Normalizing outage minutes against energy delivered changes the story. South doesn't have the most outage minutes in raw terms — East does (77,896 vs 72,807) — but South delivers less total power, so its reliability burden per kWh is worse. This is the ranking that should drive grid-maintenance priority: South, then East, then West, North, and Central.

4. A Small Number of Meters Cause Most of the Outages

The 10 worst-performing meters — concentrated in South, Central, and North — each logged 13 to 25 separate outage events, totaling 2,244 to 3,239 minutes apiece. The single worst offender, MTR0281 in South, racked up 25 events and 3,239 minutes on its own. This points to a hardware-inspection list, not a zone-wide infrastructure overhaul — fixing or replacing these ten meters would address a disproportionate share of the city's reliability problem.

5. Peak Demand Is a Weekday Story

Average peak usage on weekdays is 4.18 kWh, compared to 3.28 kWh on weekends — a 27.5% gap. That lines up with commercial and industrial activity driving the load curve, and it also means demand-response programs (encouraging large users to shift consumption) have the most room to work on weekdays, when the grid is under the most strain.

6. Central Costs the Most Per Reading — But Isn't the Highest-Usage Zone

Central has the highest average cost per reading at ₹140.37, even though North uses more total power. That's a tariff-mix effect rather than a consumption effect. It suggests cost-reduction work in Central — renegotiating tariff structure, shifting load to cheaper time windows — could deliver more rupee-for-rupee savings than usage-reduction efforts in a higher-volume zone like North.

7. There's a Mild Seasonal Pattern

Consumption dips slightly in February and climbs through March, April, and May before easing off in June — and it happens in every zone at roughly the same time. That synchrony across all five zones points to a shared external driver, most likely temperature and HVAC load, rather than anything zone-specific. Worth confirming against actual weather data in a future pass.

✅ What This Means in Practice

  • Prioritize grid maintenance in South, then East — using the normalized outage-per-kWh ratio, not raw outage-minute totals.
  • Send the 10 worst meters (starting with MTR0281) for inspection before any broader infrastructure spend.
  • Aim demand-response programs at weekdays, where the peak-usage gap actually lives.
  • Treat Central's high cost as a pricing problem, not a usage problem.
  • Don't build an Industrial-first efficiency strategy assuming it's the biggest lever — Residential is, by total volume.
📊 Data source: SmartCityEnergy.csv (72,400 meter-day readings, 400 meters, 5 zones, Jan–Jun 2025).
